\section{Objective}
The goal of this project is to develop and evaluate Quantile-Conditioned Group-Relative Policy Optimization (QC-GRPO), a generalization of GRPO that replaces the mean reward baseline with a continuum of quantile-conditioned baselines. We pursue three objectives. First, we formally derive the QC-GRPO objective, from which   majority- and minority-voting optimization emerge as special cases. Second, we introduce an adaptive curriculum over the quantile index $\alpha$, which anneals from majority- to minority-weighting in response to reward variance collapse, and demonstrate that it restores gradient signal when standard GRPO stalls. Third, we evaluate how $\alpha$ shapes the Pass@1–Pass@k tradeoff and out-of-distribution generalization across a difficulty spectrum including MATH-500, AMC, and AIME/HMMT.

Beyond algorithmic novelty, this work addresses a practical bottleneck in RLVR: diversity collapse. Standard training concentrates probability mass on a narrow solution mode, which performs well under majority voting but fails on harder, distribution-shifted problems requiring robust reasoning. QC-GRPO mitigates this by preserving the model’s ability to produce correct low-probability solutions while maintaining stable training dynamics. More broadly, it reframes the choice of baseline as a continuous design parameter interpolating between worst-case and average-case optimization.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{RLVR and the Mean-Baseline Limit}
Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) is the dominant paradigm for improving LLM reasoning, with Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) \citep{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical} serving as the primary algorithm. GRPO computes advantages relative to the group-mean reward, a design validated by DeepSeek-R1 \citep{Guo_2025}. However, mean-relative baselines implicitly optimize for majority-vote performance (Pass@1). This encourages the model to outperform its average self but provides no direct signal to improve the rare-but-correct reasoning modes found in the distribution's tail.

\subsection{Diversity-Preserving RL for Reasoning}
A recognized failure mode of standard RLVR is ``diversity collapse,'' where the model concentrates probability mass on a narrow set of easy solution modes \citep{yue2025doesreinforcementlearningreally}. Concurrent methods attempt to mitigate this by modifying the objective to favor exploration. F-GRPO \citep{plyusov2026fgrpodontletpolicy} introduces focal weighting to up-weight rare-correct trajectories at the prompt level. SetPO \citep{li2026setposetlevelpolicyoptimization} defines a kernelized set-level diversity objective to maximize the marginal contribution of unique reasoning chains. While these methods encourage variety, they do not explicitly optimize for the risk-sensitive tail of the reward distribution.

\subsection{Tail-Optimization and Pass@k}
Most relevant to our work is \textbf{Pass@K Policy Optimization (PKPO)} \citep{walder2025passkpolicyoptimizationsolving}, which reframes the training objective as a tail-optimization problem. PKPO demonstrates that annealing the training objective from Pass@1 (mean) toward Pass@k (the tail) prevents solution collapse and recovers gradient signal on difficult reasoning tasks where correct answers are sparse. However, PKPO relies on discrete values of $k$ and fixed, non-reactive annealing schedules. Furthermore, existing tail-optimization methods result in a single static policy, lacking a mechanism for inference-time steering between conservative and exploratory reasoning modes.

Test-Time Reinforcement Learning \citep{zuo2025ttrltesttimereinforcementlearning} closes the loop between inference-time aggregation and training by using majority-voted pseudo-labels as reward signals for self-improvement on unlabeled data. QC-GRPO is, to our knowledge, the first method to apply a CVaR-style tail objective directly to the within-group correctness distribution in RLVR, without requiring distributional value estimation or a separate risk model.
\section{Technical Outline}
\subsection{Approach}

Standard GRPO computes the advantage of a rollout $y_i$ within a group $\{y_1,\dots,y_N\}$ as $\hat{A}(y_i) = r_i - \bar{r}$, where $\bar{r}$ is the group mean reward. This implicitly optimizes majority-vote performance. We generalize this by replacing the mean with a quantile-conditioned baseline $b_\alpha$, defined as the $\alpha$-quantile of the within-group reward distribution:
\[
\hat{A}_\alpha(y_i) = r_i - b_\alpha(\{r_1,\dots,r_N\}).
\]

Setting $\alpha=0.5$ recovers standard GRPO (up to mean vs.\ median). As $\alpha \to 0$, the objective rewards any rollout that outperforms the worst-performing samples, encouraging correct but rare solutions (i.e., minority-voting optimization).

We train with a mixture of quantiles:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_\alpha w(\alpha)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{A}_\alpha(y_i)\log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)\right],
\]
where $w(\alpha)$ defines the quantile weighting. Top-heavy $w$ approximates best-of-$N$ training, while bottom-heavy $w$ emphasizes tail performance.

Our key contribution is an adaptive curriculum over $\alpha$: $w(\alpha)$ shifts from high to low quantiles during training. The shift is triggered by collapse in reward variance, reallocating weight toward lower quantiles to recover gradient signal from rare correct rollouts.

\subsection{Evaluation Plan}

We evaluate QC-GRPO on reasoning performance and generalization under distribution shift.

\paragraph{Baselines:}
We compare against GRPO, DAPO, and SetPO, along with fixed-$\alpha$ variants ($\alpha \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}$).

\paragraph{Metrics:}
We report Pass@1 and Pass@$k$ ($k \in \{4,16,64\}$) to measure the tradeoff between majority accuracy and solution diversity. We expect low-$\alpha$ training to improve Pass@$k$ (especially on hard tasks), with a small Pass@1 tradeoff that is mitigated by the learned curriculum.

\paragraph{Benchmarks:}
We train on standard math reasoning data and evaluate across difficulty: MATH-500 (in-distribution), AMC (moderate OOD), and AIME/HMMT (hard OOD). We hypothesize that lower $\alpha$ improves performance on hard OOD problems, where correct solutions are rare.

\paragraph{Ablations:}
We study fixed vs.\ learned $w(\alpha)$, with vs.\ without $\alpha$-conditioning in the model, and variance-triggered vs.\ schedule-based curricula.
