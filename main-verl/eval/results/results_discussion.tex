\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We evaluate all three trained arms (GRPO, Minority-CoT, Poly-EPO) against an
untrained base model (Qwen3-4B-Base) along two axes: \emph{answer coverage}
(pass@$k$) and \emph{reasoning diversity} (CoT diversity@$k$). All metrics use
the exact unbiased estimator averaged over all $\binom{n}{k}$ subsets of $k$
rollouts from $n = 64$ total per prompt, so results are not sensitive to rollout
ordering.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Pass@\texorpdfstring{$k$}{k} on OOD Benchmarks}
\label{sec:results:passk}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We measure pass@$k$ on three out-of-distribution (OOD) benchmarks: AIME 2025
(30 problems), AIME 2026 (30 problems), and BeyondAIME (100 problems). These
benchmarks were not seen during training on Polaris and test generalization to
harder competition-style mathematics.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:passk} and Figure~\ref{fig:passk}, all trained arms
regress substantially on OOD benchmarks relative to the untrained base model.
On BeyondAIME at pass@16, the base model (0.144) outperforms the best trained
arm by approximately $2.5\times$ (GRPO/Poly-EPO $\approx 0.058$). Crucially,
this gap does not close as $k$ increases: the pass@$k$ curves in
Figure~\ref{fig:passk} remain roughly parallel on a log scale from $k=1$ to
$k=64$, indicating that the trained models are not exploring solution strategies
that the base model finds with more samples.

Among the trained arms, GRPO and Poly-EPO perform comparably on BeyondAIME
(pass@16: 0.057 vs.\ 0.058), with Minority-CoT slightly behind (0.043). The
set-RL arms do not improve over standard GRPO despite being specifically designed
to find rare correct solutions. Results on the 30-problem AIME splits are too
noisy for reliable arm comparisons.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{CoT Diversity on MATH-500 and BeyondAIME}
\label{sec:results:cot}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Pass@$k$ on single-answer integer benchmarks conflates correctness with solution
diversity: if a model gets a problem right, all correct rollouts give the same
answer, so distinct-correct-answer clusters is always 0 or 1 per problem. To
measure whether the set-RL arms generate genuinely diverse \emph{reasoning
strategies}, we use an LLM judge (Qwen3-4B-Instruct) to cluster each rollout
by macro- and micro-strategy~\citep{polyepo}. Our metric is the average number
of distinct correct CoT clusters per problem, computed over problems the model
solved at least once (using all 64 rollouts). We evaluate on MATH-500
(500 in-distribution problems) and BeyondAIME (100 OOD problems).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Distinct correct CoT clusters per solved problem on MATH-500 and
BeyondAIME. Averaged only over problems with at least one correct rollout,
using all 64 rollouts. Best per dataset in \textbf{bold}.}
\label{tab:cot}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\begin{tabular}{llcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Dataset} & \textbf{Arm} & \textbf{\#solved / total} & \textbf{distinct correct CoT clusters} \\
\midrule
\multirow{4}{*}{MATH-500}
  & Base (Qwen3-4B) & \textbf{464} / 500 & \textbf{1.179} \\
  & GRPO            & 408 / 500 & 1.152 \\
  & Poly-EPO        & 405 / 500 & 1.034 \\
  & Minority-CoT    & 402 / 500 & 0.990 \\
\midrule
\multirow{4}{*}{BeyondAIME}
  & Base (Qwen3-4B) & \textbf{29} / 100 & 0.586 \\
  & GRPO            & 12 / 100 & \textbf{0.667} \\
  & Minority-CoT    & 9 / 100  & 0.333 \\
  & Poly-EPO        & 13 / 100 & 0.231 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:cot} and Figures~\ref{fig:cot}--\ref{fig:scatter} reveal two
distinct patterns. On MATH-500 (Table~\ref{tab:cot}, top), the base model
(1.179) leads narrowly over GRPO (1.152), with the set-RL arms below (Poly-EPO
1.034, Minority-CoT 0.990). All four arms generate roughly one distinct correct
reasoning approach per solved problem, reflecting that most MATH-500 problems
have a single canonical solution method.

On BeyondAIME (Table~\ref{tab:cot}, bottom), conditioning on solved problems
reverses the ordering: GRPO (0.667) exceeds the base model (0.586), while the
set-RL arms fall well below both (Minority-CoT 0.333, Poly-EPO 0.231). The
set-RL arms rank last on the metric they were explicitly trained to maximize.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{RL Training Reduces OOD Generalization and Reasoning Diversity}
\label{sec:discussion:collapse}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Pass@$k$ (Figure~\ref{fig:passk}) shows a clear and consistent story: RL
training reduces OOD correctness for all objectives. The base model dominates
at every $k$ on every OOD benchmark. The per-problem CoT diversity result
(Figure~\ref{fig:cot}) is more nuanced. On in-distribution MATH-500, the base
model (1.179) leads narrowly over GRPO (1.152). On OOD BeyondAIME, however,
GRPO (0.667) actually \emph{exceeds} the base model (0.586) in per-problem
reasoning diversity, suggesting that RL training need not suppress diverse
solution strategies among the problems a model can solve --- it primarily
reduces the number of problems it can solve at all.

Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} illustrates this: on BeyondAIME (right panel),
GRPO sits \emph{above} base in the diversity direction despite solving fewer
problems. The tradeoff RL training induces is primarily one of
\textbf{breadth} (fewer problems solved) rather than \textbf{depth} (less
diverse reasoning per problem).

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Set-RL Objectives Do Not Improve over GRPO}
\label{sec:discussion:setrl}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The central hypothesis motivating this work is that objectives which explicitly
reward solution diversity (Minority-CoT, Poly-EPO) should produce models that
explore more reasoning strategies at inference time. Our results do not support
this hypothesis. On both MATH-500 and BeyondAIME, the set-RL arms rank below
standard GRPO on per-problem CoT diversity (Table~\ref{tab:cot},
Figure~\ref{fig:cot}). On BeyondAIME, the gap is large: GRPO (0.667) vs.
Minority-CoT (0.333) and Poly-EPO (0.231). On OOD pass@$k$, GRPO and Poly-EPO
are statistically indistinguishable (BeyondAIME pass@16: 0.057 vs.\ 0.058),
with Minority-CoT slightly behind (Figure~\ref{fig:summary}).

The set-RL training signal --- rewarding diverse correct solutions within each
batch --- does not translate into a measurable diversity advantage at evaluation
time. One possible explanation is that the diversity reward is satisfied
\emph{during training} by surface variation in rollouts (e.g., different
intermediate steps or notation) rather than by genuinely distinct macro-strategies.
If the LLM judge clusters such variations together at test time (as it should,
given the strict clustering criteria), the diversity seen during training does
not appear in our eval metric.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Minority-CoT Appears to Actively Reduce Diversity}
\label{sec:discussion:minority}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Among trained arms, Minority-CoT consistently achieves the \emph{lowest}
per-problem CoT diversity on both benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:cot},
Figure~\ref{fig:cot}). On BeyondAIME, Minority-CoT (0.333) is less than half
of GRPO (0.667). This is a surprising inversion: the objective designed to
maximize correct-cluster diversity produces the least diverse model.

A likely mechanism is a mode-collapsing effect of the minority voting objective.
Minority-CoT rewards rollouts that reach the correct answer via the
\emph{least common reasoning cluster} in each batch. If the model learns to
reliably produce the ``minority mode'' answer --- a specific rare-but-correct
approach to each problem type --- it may converge toward a narrow distribution
concentrated around that mode, suppressing other valid strategies that would
otherwise appear with moderate probability. In effect, the objective replaces
one mode (the common answer) with another (the minority answer), rather than
broadening the distribution.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Diversity Reduction Is Not an Artifact of Lower Correctness}
\label{sec:discussion:confound}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

A potential confound is that trained arms solve fewer problems, giving fewer
problems over which to measure diversity. The MATH-500 data rules this out. All
four arms solve between 402 and 464 out of 500 problems --- a narrow 13\% range
--- yet span 0.19 units in per-problem CoT diversity (0.990--1.179). As shown
in Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} (left panel), the three trained arms occupy nearly
identical $x$-positions (pass@64 $\approx 0.80$--$0.93$) while spreading
across 0.16 units vertically. The diversity reduction in the set-RL arms
reflects a genuine change in the distribution over solution strategies, not an
artifact of solving fewer problems.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Implications and Future Directions}
\label{sec:discussion:implications}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Our results show that, as implemented here, set-RL objectives (Minority-CoT,
Poly-EPO) trained on Polaris problems with a 4B base model do not achieve their
stated goal of improving reasoning diversity. Two directions seem most promising
for future work:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\arabic*.]

\item \textbf{Training distribution.}
Polaris problems may not provide sufficient natural answer diversity to generate
a useful minority signal. Problems with multiple structurally distinct solution
approaches (e.g., combinatorics problems solvable by direct counting, generating
functions, or recursion) could yield a richer cluster structure during training.
Filtering the training set for problems with high judge-measured within-prompt
diversity in base model rollouts may also help.

\item \textbf{Objective formulation.}
The current cluster reward may be gamed by surface variation rather than
strategic diversity. Strengthening the judge prompt to penalize superficially
different but strategically identical rollouts, or introducing an answer-level
diversity constraint in addition to the CoT cluster reward, could better align
the training signal with the desired generalization behavior.

\end{enumerate}

More broadly, our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence that RL
training for math reasoning trades generalization for reliability. Whether this
tradeoff is fundamental or addressable through better training data and
objectives is an open question.
